## The Basics

First, a spoiler. When you defeat the Naughty Sorceress, who lives in that big tower on the right hand side of the map, you'll then break a Prism, and an Astral Rift will open up, allowing you to "Ascend". When you do so, you get to restart the game, returning to level 1 with all of your stuff reset (until you break the prism again), and you can do it all over again. Sorry if you didn't know that yet. It's kind of vital to what we're going to get into here.

Speedrunning a (nominal) MMO is a bit of a weird concept, but fortunately in Kingdom of Loathing it's well defined. Just like how in a normal game, a speedrun time would be measured as the time taken between starting the game and reaching the credits; a Kingdom of Loathing speedrun is measured in terms of the **days taken** and **turns played** (or **adventures spent**, the two are interchangeable) at the moment that you smash the Prism. When you're going fast, you want to be getting both of those numbers as low as possible, prioritising days over turns- a speedrun that takes 3 days and 400 turns (3/400) would be considered slower than a run that took 2 days and 500 turns (2/500)[^2].

As a result of that, there are two primary things you want to keep in mind: **turn-bloat**, and **turn-cutting**.

**Turn-bloat** is what helps you take as few days as possible to finish your run (or "make daycount"). The general idea of turnbloat is generating as many adventures as possible each day: eating high quality food, drinking high quality booze, using Milk of Magnesium, using The Ode to Booze, equipping gear that gives you more adventures over rollover; anything that lets you play more turns in a day is turnbloat.

**Turn-cutting** is the other side of the same coin- completing the main quest of the game while spending as few adventures as possible. As a rule, this is a lot harder than turn-bloating, and it will rarely if ever save you entire days, but it will save you turns.

These two concepts lead us to the golden rule of speed ascension:

**Every single thing you do should either save adventures, or generate adventures.**

Technically, this is something of an oversimplification. It is more accurate to say that every single resource you spend should be used to either advance the main quest as fast as possible or help you to do so, or should be used to generate the turns you need to get there. With this golden rule in mind, let's look at the resources everyone has access to.

## Resources

**Adventures** are your first big obvious resource. You get forty-and-a-bit of them every day, and there's thousands of foods and boozes you can use to turn your liver and stomach space into them. What do you use them for? Adventuring. As the "core" resource of the game, every adventure you spend wants to be, ideally, on the critical path of the main quest (getting you quest progress, either by completing quests or leveling up to unlock new quests) or generating adventures.[^3]

**Stomach and Liver Space** are the next important resources to talk about. You can eat fifteen fullness of food per day, and drink fourteen potency-worth of booze before you become falling-down drunk. There's no real tricks here to speak of initially, you just want to fill your stomach with the best food you can, your liver with the best booze you can, and save an extremely alcoholic nightcap to drink as the last thing you do in the day. All stuff that the Toot Oriole taught you.

What constitutes "the best food and booze you can" changes super rapidly with permanently learned ("permed") skills (hey, surprise, every time you finish a run you get to pick a skill you learned that run and keep it for all future ascensions), so broad advice doesn't help much here. (Although you can find more specific advice about skills in [this perm list](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1UG68Vl_5V-EqQXdyF2QMehdZ43Gh78pwD3R0UC_bky4/edit?gid=330400182#gid=330400182), which is kept up-to-date by Scotch and others.) Just remember that the Dry Noodles produced by the Pastamastery skill that Pastamancers can learn from the guild trainer (and then permanently learn in order to bring it into all future runs) can be cooked with a bunch of items that you'll find lying around while going about the main quest, make sure to prioritise finding those, and you'll be fine for the time being.

**Lucky Adventures**. Now we're into the fun stuff. Every day, the Hermit who lives in the Big Mountains will sell you up to three eleven-leaf clovers in return for worthless trinkets (try using chewing gum on a string, purchased from the general store, if you want to find trinkets). When you use these clovers, the next adventure you go on will be guaranteed to be a "Lucky Adventure", a special kind of adventure unique to the location you adventured in.

Lucky Adventures are important to think about and plan around because you (usually) only have three of them available per day, and they are extremely powerful and flexible. For a brief selection, depending on where you adventure, you can use lucky adventures to…

- Get you the weapon you need to defeat the Naughty Sorceress
- Get you the ore the Trapper is asking for without having to find a mining outfit and go mining.
- Get you fasteners and wood to build the bridge over the Smut Orc Chasm
- Get a bunch of turns of Ultrahydrated to explore the Arid Desert
- Get you A-Boo clues for the spooky peak
- Get you extremely powerful food and booze
- Get you into a fight for stone wool to quickly discover the secrets of the Hidden City
- Get you a potion that gives you a huge buff to basically any modifier you care to mention

Determining how you use your limited supply of clovers is a huge part of turn cutting when you're learning the game, and it will remain a powerful tool as you develop, so getting a handle on it early is very worthwhile.

You might ask yourself why that last bullet point is there- after all, potions of cold damage or hot resistance neither generate turns nor save them, so why would you spend resources on them? Well...

## Quests and Modifier Pumping

As a rule, nearly every quest in KoL boils down to "Adventure in a zone until something happens". Sometimes, this is extremely direct- the game says "You have to spend N turns here, then the quest is completed". One example of this kind of quest is decoding the map to Cobb's Knob. You spend ten turns there, then you find the code. Easy enough.

Usually, though, it's slightly more subtle than that, and in those situations there's usually a modifier (the little blue text you can find when you click on equipment and effects) you can try to get as high as possible to speed it up and cut turns. By way of example, one of the most common quest types in KoL is "adventure in this zone until you get a non-combat adventure". If you want to speed that up, you want to get as many effects and equips with blue text on them that says "Monsters are less attracted to you", in order to get that non-combat adventure as fast as possible. The next most common is "adventure here until a specific item drops"- so in that case, you want to get your item drop bonus as high as possible.

Between them, the noncombat rate (what speedrunners call "monsters are less attracted to you") and item drop modifiers account for most of the quests in the game, and you'll rarely go wrong getting them as high as possible at all times, but some specific steps of other quests have slightly weirder modifiers that make them go faster, and knowing what those are helps a lot for (among other things) determining which Lucky Adventures you want to prioritise.

A good example for this early on is the Haunted Kitchen. In the Haunted Kitchen, you're searching for the key to the Haunted Billiards Room. Once you've opened 21 drawers in the Haunted Kitchen, the next time you win a fight in the kitchen, you'll find the key you're looking for. So far so good. Ordinarily, you open one drawer every time you win a fight in the Haunted Kitchen, so that's 21 adventures to open the drawers and 1 to find the key, for a total of 22 adventures spent.

However, you can speed that up. Every fight you win in the kitchen, the game flips a coin. Heads, it looks at your hot resistance. Tails, it looks at your stench resistance. Up to +9 resistance, for every +3 resistance you have to the element it chose, you search an extra drawer, up to a maximum of four drawers searched per fight. That means if you have nine hot resistance and nine stench resistance, you're searching four drawers per adventure- 6 adventures to open drawers and 1 to find a key, for a total of seven. _Much_ better.

There's quite a few quests like this, wanting you to pump up an attribute other than item drop or noncombat rate. An incomplete list of the zones, and the attributes they're looking for, follows:

- Typical Tavern Basement wants Monster Level and Elemental Damage of all types except Sleaze[^4]
- Cyrpt Cranny wants Monster Level[^5]
- Cyrpt Alcove wants Combat Initiative[^6]
- Lair of the Ninja Snowman wants _Combat_ Rate (_not_ Noncombat Rate- it's a weird one)
- Smut Orc Logging Camp wants you to deal lots of cold damage, then buff up Sleaze Resistance if you're a Moxie class, Spell Damage if you're a Mysticality class, or Weapon Damage if you're a Muscle class.
- A-Boo Peak wants Maximum HP, Spooky Resistance, and Cold Resistance
- Twins Peak wants Stench Resistance and Combat Initiative
- Oil Peak wants Monster Level
- The Black Forest wants Combat Rate (another weirdo)
- Haunted Kitchen wants Stench Resistance and Hot Resistance
- Haunted Boiler Room wants Monster Level
- Zeppelin Mob wants Sleaze Damage and Sleaze Spell Damage
- Sonofa Beach wants Combat Rate (one final weirdo)
- Themthar Hills wants Meat Drop
- The 8-Bit Realm wants a rotation of Initiative, Damage Absorption, Item Drop, and Meat Drop
- The Wall of Meat wants Meat Drop

…and finally, there are three Naughty Sorceress Contests, which are randomly determined. The first one requires Combat Initiative, the second requires you to pump up one of your "offstats" (out of Moxie, Muscle and Mysticality- for example, if you're a Moxie class, it will either be Mysticality or Muscle), and the third requires Elemental Damage and Spell Damage of a specific random element.

Additionally, while item drop is always good, there's a few places where it's extra good- The Haunted Laundry Room, Haunted Wine Cellar, the Hippy Orchard, the Middle Chamber of the Pyramid, the Cyrpt Nook and the Penultimate Fantasy Airship all respond very well to a little extra item drop if you can spare it.

There is one more modifier that is almost always good: Familiar Weight. However, we're out of time for today, and if I go on much longer this article will be absurdly long, so in lieu of talking about that right now, we'll pick up with _why_ familiar weight is great next time. Until then, adieu!
